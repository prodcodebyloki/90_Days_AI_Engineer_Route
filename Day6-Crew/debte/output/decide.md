After weighing the arguments presented on both sides of the debate regarding the motion "AI will do more good than harm to society," it is clear that the arguments against the motion are more compelling.

The proponent of the motion emphasized the potential benefits of AI in sectors such as healthcare, environmental sustainability, the economy, inclusivity, and the opportunity for ethical governance. While these points highlight important advancements and possible positive impacts of AI, they tend to be largely optimistic and somewhat idealistic. The speaker did acknowledge the challenges that come with AI but suggested that these challenges could be managed through responsible development and regulation. However, this reliance on future governance frameworks to mitigate risks does not address the immediate and pressing harms associated with AI.

In contrast, the arguments presented against the motion outlined significant and tangible risks of AI that are currently affecting society. First, the critique on how AI can exacerbate existing inequalities and reinforce biases is particularly powerful. The potential for harmful algorithms to perpetuate discrimination in critical areas is a pressing concern that cannot be overlooked. Moreover, the argument around job displacement presents a realistic and urgent challenge that many workers are already facing with automation. This is a stark contrast to the more optimistic view that new job categories will seamlessly replace lost ones.

The centralization of power in AI development, as pointed out by the opponent, raises essential ethical concerns about accountability and control. The argument that a few tech companies dictate the trajectory of AI development poses serious implications for individual autonomy and societal structure, representing a genuine source of concern that consumers and regulators must navigate.

Lastly, the existential threats posed by autonomous weapons and the militarization of AI are critical issues that invoke a serious discourse on safety and ethics. This point enhances the argument against the motion by highlighting an area where AI could lead to catastrophic outcomes if not properly managed.

In conclusion, while both sides presented important arguments regarding the potential for AI to impact society, the risks highlighted by the opposing side—particularly around inequality, job displacement, accountability, and existential threats—are more pressing and immediate. The potential harms presented are not just theoretical but indicate real-time consequences that society is already grappling with. Therefore, based purely on the merits of the arguments presented, I find the case against the motion to be more convincing.
